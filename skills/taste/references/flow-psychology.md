# Flow psychology — consult when building onboarding, signup, paywall, upgrade, or pricing screens

Six behavioral principles for screens where the goal is completion/conversion, not aesthetics.
Source: youtu.be/2TlIg3VokY8. These are framing/sequencing decisions, not visual direction —
apply alongside whatever preset is committed, don't treat as a preset itself.

## Smart defaults
Don't hand the user a blank form. Pre-fill every field with the most common choice; the task
becomes "scan and adjust" instead of "fill from scratch." 70-90% of users never change defaults —
a default reads as a recommendation, not laziness.

## Goal-gradient effect (never start at zero)
People accelerate as they approach a finish line — and you control where the line starts. Find
something the user already did (account created, one field filled) and count it as progress
instead of showing 0%. A profile-strength or progress meter should never open at zero.

## Reciprocity (give before you ask)
Don't gate the first result behind signup ("create an account to see your report"). Give a real,
useful partial result first (score, top issues), then offer the full version as the upsell. The
free sample creates the pull to reciprocate — asking first creates a wall.

## IKEA / endowment effect (build before you ask to save)
Let the user make choices (name, style, goal) before the signup wall appears, and label the CTA
"Continue" not "Sign up." Something they built or chose feels owned; abandoning it feels like a
loss, not skipping a form.

## Loss framing beats gain framing
Loss is ~2x more motivating than an equivalent gain (Kahneman). For an upgrade/retention prompt,
don't sell what they'd gain — show what they're about to lose (their actual files, by name, with
a countdown), and make the dismiss option cost something ("I'll risk it," not "Maybe later").

## Contrast effect (anchor before you show the price)
A price shown in isolation gets evaluated on its own; shown right after a larger number, it
shrinks by comparison ($50/mo add-on look tiny right under a $1900 cart, framed as "2.6%"). Never
show a cost in isolation — control what number the user sees immediately before it.
