## 1. Principles

**P1 — Past behavior, not future intention (Fitzpatrick).** People don't lie about what they
did; they lie systematically about what they would do. "We'd definitely consider it" is
noise; "last week I did X" is signal. Anchor every question to a real event that already
happened.

**P2 — The brain doesn't know how it works (Kahneman, System 1/2).** Ask "how do you decide
X?" and you get a post-hoc rationalization, not the real process — System 1 isn't verbally
accessible. Fix: trace a specific recent case; the process emerges from the narration, not
from the description.

**P3 — Policy questions invite façade answers.** "Do you have a shared criterion for
prioritizing leads?" → expected answer: "Yes, of course." Zero information. Abstract
questions invite the person to answer as they wish things worked, not as they actually work.

**P4 — Ask "how" and "what"; avoid bare "why" (Voss).** "Why don't you use the CRM?" is
accusatory and breeds defensiveness. "How does it work today when a lead comes in?" is
neutral, curious, and makes the other person do the work. (In Italian, use "come mai"
sparingly for the same reason — "perché" lands as an accusation.)

**P5 — Humble Inquiry: you're asking because you don't know, not to confirm (Schein).**
Posture matters as much as the question. A senior person instantly feels whether you're
gathering evidence for something you've already decided. Authentic questions — ones you
genuinely don't know the answer to — disarm hierarchy and signal you're there to understand,
not to evaluate.

**P6 — Climb the implications before proposing solutions (Rackham, SPIN).** Once a problem
(P) surfaces, don't jump to a solution: deepen the consequences (I — Implication). "What
happens when that information isn't there?" makes the problem concrete, urgent, costly. The
client articulates the pain themselves — more effective than any external diagnosis.

**P7 — Start from strengths, then descend into problems (Cooperrider, AI).** Especially with
a senior person: opening with "what already works well in this flow?" lowers the guard and
makes it easier to talk about what doesn't. It's not diplomacy — it's sequence. People are
more honest about gaps after they've named what they value.

**P8 — The concrete artifact beats the verbal description (Contextual Inquiry).** "What do you
use to track leads?" has limited value. "Can you show me the spreadsheet you typically use?"
or "if you had to find yesterday's lead, where would you look?" — the real artifact carries
more truth in 30 seconds than any description.

**P9 — Resistance is information (Block).** If an answer is vague, evasive, or changes the
subject, don't push. Note the area — it's often the point of maximum relevance. Return
laterally later: "earlier you mentioned X — how does that work in practice?"

**P10 — Climb to the root, not the symptom (Toyota, 5 Whys).** The first stated problem is
almost never the real one. Each answer opens "and when that happens, what happens next?" or
"what causes that situation?" — two or three levels down sits the real constraint. Cap at
~3 levels in one conversation.

**P11 — Open as an apprentice, not an expert (Schein / Contextual Inquiry).** Master–apprentice
model: you're the apprentice, they're the expert on their own process. Deliberately signaling
not-knowing ("I want to understand how it really works in your specific context — I take
nothing for granted") is more effective than displaying competence.

**P12 — Visibility questions reveal the gap without accusing.** "If you wanted to know X right
now, where would you look, and how long would it take?" doesn't say "you have no data." It
makes the other person realize it themselves, in the first person, in the moment of answering.

## 2. Toolbox (T1–T10)

**T1 — Trace the last real case.** *"Let's take a concrete recent case — [type of event].
What exactly happened?"* — Opener for any area. Replaces all abstract-process questions.
(Fitzpatrick + Kahneman.)

**T2 — Visibility probe.** *"If you wanted to know [specific X] right now, where would you look?
How long would it take?"* — Reveals absence of data/visibility without accusing.
(Contextual Inquiry + Fitzpatrick.)

**T3 — Calibrated how/what opener.** *"How does it work today when [specific event]?" / "What
exactly happens after [trigger]?"* — Maps the real flow without loading the question. (Voss.)

**T4 — Implication ladder.** *"And when [problem] happens, what does that actually cause?" →
"How often does it happen?" → "And what effect does that have on [business outcome]?"* — After
they name a problem. Don't propose solutions until you've climbed the ladder. (Rackham, SPIN.)

**T5 — Artifact anchor.** *"Can you show me [doc/sheet/system] you use at that moment?" / "What's
typically written in that sheet?"* — When the verbal description is vague; the artifact drips
detail. (Beyer & Holtzblatt.)

**T6 — Best-case opener.** *"In this flow, what already works well — where are you satisfied with
how you work?"* — Opener, especially with seniors. Reduces defensiveness, builds useful contrast
with the gaps. (Cooperrider, AI.)

**T7 — Mirror (echo).** Repeat the last 2–3 words as an implicit question: *"...we can never know
it." → "You can't know it?"* — When you want them to continue without leading them; the next
detail surfaces spontaneously. (Voss.)

**T8 — Exception question.** *"When it went particularly well — was there a case where you had
full visibility into what was happening? How did you pull that off?"* — Finds existing islands
of competence and the preconditions for things working. (Appreciative Inquiry + contextual.)

**T9 — Loss archaeology.** *"It occurs to me to ask — was there a recent lead, a deal, you feel
you lost that with better information you'd have handled differently?"* — Surfaces the cost of
the problem without naming it. They tell the story themselves. (Fitzpatrick + Rackham.)

**T10 — Root-cause ladder.** *"What causes that situation?" → "And why does it work that way?"
(reframed as "what leads to X?")* — You've found a symptom and want the root. Max 3 levels in
one interview. (5 Whys + Schein.)

## 3. Sequencing & framing

**Opening (5–10 min).** Frame the format: "This isn't an audit, it's a mapping — I want to
understand how you actually work, not how it should work in theory." First question: **T6**
(best-case opener) — don't start from problems. Establish the implicit contract: "Any answer is
useful, including 'I don't know' or 'we don't have that.'"

**Body — map the real flow (20–30 min).** Follow one concrete recent case from birth to close
(or loss) as the thread. Sequence: **T1 → T3 → T2 → T4 → T7** (mirror when you want depth without
pushing). Every time they name a gap: don't comment, don't propose — apply **T4** (implication
ladder).

**Descent into the gaps (10–15 min).** Use **T9** (loss archaeology) to surface the cost without
naming it yourself. **T10** (root cause) on 1–2 areas at most — don't overload.

**Closing.** Reflect back what you heard (Motivational Interviewing summary) — let them
correct/confirm; raises ownership. Closing question: "Is there something you think I should have
asked and didn't?" Don't list solutions. Say what you'll do with the information.

**With a senior person (CEO) in particular.** Don't use jargon (CRM, workflow, KPI) in your
questions unless they use it first. Don't ask questions that imply they should already have that
answer. Prefer "help me understand" over "why don't you…" — signals apprentice posture. If they're
impatient or terse, compress to 3 core questions (T1, T2, T9) and let them lead.

**Worldview / identity / public-body-of-work interviews (essays, books, a personal site, a public
mission).** Distilled from several 2026-07-16 writing-scope sessions; consolidated here (not
siloed in one skill) so any elicitation skill benefits.

- **Elicit the worldview before the artifact.** Don't open by optimizing the first essay/article/
  section. First read the relevant scope docs and public artifacts, synthesize the methodology
  back briefly, open a checkpoint, then ask one concrete origin-story question at a time: what
  systems they criticize and why, the positive alternative they believe in (distinguish the enemy
  from the mechanism — e.g. "not anti-capitalism, anti-extraction"), the personal experience that
  earns the claim, and the effect they want the reader/audience to feel. Only after that propose
  article order, sections, titles, or publishing sequence.
- **A stated "missing layer" (e.g. life aims) blocks production strategy until resolved.** If the
  human says the real gap is something upstream of the artifact — life aims, mission, positioning —
  stop and elicit that first; don't produce an editorial map, outline, or draft in the meantime.
  Use open, non-anchored questions ("what are you ultimately trying to build?", not "what does
  success look like at 40?"); record genuine uncertainty as an open-flag rather than inventing a
  strategy to fill the gap.
- **Stop drift the moment the human says it's going too far.** Distribution/channel questions
  (social platforms, face-on-camera, publishing cadence) are downstream of the worldview and easy
  to over-explore. The moment the human flags drift: checkpoint the current answer, freeze the
  undecided item as an open-flag, state the narrower working default, and return to the artifact
  that motivated the interview.
- **Don't re-ask what the conversation already answered.** Before the next question, check whether
  prior answers already imply it; if so, state the synthesis and let the human correct it instead
  of forcing a restate.
- **Keep the essay/content ethics boundary in view when distribution comes up:** avoid guru
  content, rage bait, status signaling, fake certainty, or funnels that monetize confusion — income
  should come from useful systems/companies, not from selling enlightenment.

## 4. Pitfalls

- **Hypotheticals:** "If you had a better system, would you use it?" → Useless. Everyone says yes.
- **Policy/procedure questions:** "Do you have a defined process for X?" → "Yes" / "We're working
  on it." Zero information.
- **Double-barreled:** "How do you handle leads and how do you set priorities?" → They answer one;
  you don't know which.
- **Leading questions:** "I imagine without data follow-up is hard, right?" → Forces a yes.
  Confirms your frame, not their reality.
- **Bare "why":** "Why don't you use the CRM?" → Sounds like an accusation. Translate to "how did
  it end up being used less than expected?" or "what happened over time with the CRM?"
- **Jargon test:** if a question contains "process", "workflow", "KPI", "traceability",
  "pipeline" — rewrite it in plain language before sending.
- **Exposing a gap too directly:** "So you have no visibility into anything after first contact?"
  → Defensive. Use **T2** instead — they do the math themselves.
- **A chain of short questions:** a list of 15 consecutive questions wears the person down and
  signals checklist, not conversation. Three well-sequenced questions beat 15 mediocre ones.

## Quick-rewrite rule

To fix an abstract question: take any policy question and ask *"when did it last happen?"* — that's
the question to ask. If the obvious answer is "yes/no" or "more or less," the question is still too
abstract. If the answer requires telling a story, you're on the right track.
